# MyPuzzleInventory: The Discogs of Jigsaw Puzzles. Find Any Puzzle. Track What You Own. Buy What You Don't.

## The first catalog built for puzzle collectors — search thousands of puzzles, track your collection, and never buy a duplicate again.

**Minneapolis, MN — May 20, 2026** — MyPuzzleInventory today launched the first catalog built for serious puzzle collectors — search thousands of puzzles, track your collection, and never buy a duplicate again.

Megan owns 84 puzzles. She has accidentally bought two duplicates. Her wishlist lives across a Pinterest board, a Notes app, and screenshots scattered across her phone. When she sees a puzzle she likes on Instagram, she has to leave, search across three retailer sites to find where it's sold, and rely on memory to check whether she already owns it. Half the time she's not sure, so she either abandons the purchase or takes the risk.

She is not unusual. She is the typical serious puzzle collector.

With MyPuzzleInventory, Megan searches for any puzzle by title, brand, artist, or piece count. When she finds a puzzle she owns, it shows up with an "In Your Collection" badge — automatically. When she finds one she wants, she adds it to her wishlist and clicks through to buy it directly. When a family member asks what puzzles to get her for her birthday, she shares a link.

> "There's no Discogs for puzzles. No central place where you can search everything, know what you own, and actually buy it. We're building that."
> — Andrew, Founder, MyPuzzleInventory

### How It Works

1. Search for any puzzle — by title, brand, piece count, artist, or theme
2. Hit "Own," "Want," or "Completed" on any puzzle
3. Browse while seeing "You already own this" warnings in real time
4. Click "Buy" on any wishlist item — routed directly to a retailer
5. Share your collection or wishlist at `mypuzzleinventory.com/u/yourname`

> "I've bought the same Ravensburger twice. I literally just needed something to tell me 'you have this.'"
> — Megan T., puzzle collector, 80+ puzzles

### Getting Started

MyPuzzleInventory is free. Create an account at mypuzzleinventory.com, search for the first puzzle you own, and hit "Own." Your collection is yours — export it to CSV anytime.

## Customer FAQ

### Q: How is this different from IPDb, which already has 39,000+ puzzles, image recognition, and it's free?

A: IPDb is a great volunteer-built database. MyPuzzleInventory is built around a different goal: the moment you see a puzzle and need to know in 10 seconds whether you own it — and if not, buy it without leaving. IPDb has no buy links and never will; their model doesn't support it. Our catalog is actively maintained in-house, not crowdsourced, so coverage of the brands you actually buy grows consistently. If you want a database to browse, IPDb is fine. If you want a tool built around your collecting workflow, that's us.

### Q: How many puzzles are actually in the catalog at launch? If I search for my Cobble Hill from 2019, will it be there?

A: At launch, MyPuzzleInventory covers thousands of puzzles from the major brands most collectors buy — Ravensburger, Buffalo Games, Cobble Hill, and White Mountain. If you own puzzles from those brands, the hit rate on day one should be very high. For puzzles from smaller or indie brands, we're building fast — and if you search for something we don't have yet, you'll see a "Help us add this puzzle" prompt. Suggest it, and we'll prioritize it. Your collection is never blocked by a gap in the catalog.

### Q: What happens to my collection data if you shut down?

A: Your data is yours, always. Every puzzle in your collection and wishlist can be exported to CSV at any time — no premium tier required, no hoops to jump through. If MyPuzzleInventory ever shut down, you'd have your complete collection in a spreadsheet within 60 seconds. We also publish our data format openly so your export is readable by any future tool, not just ours.

### Q: The "Buy" button — where does it actually go? Am I being pushed toward one retailer because you have an affiliate deal with them?

A: Every puzzle page shows all the retailers that carry it — sorted by availability, not by who pays us more. Click whichever works for you, or ignore the links entirely; your collection tracking works either way. We do earn a small referral fee on purchases made through our links — that's what keeps the catalog free.

### Q: I already track my collection in a spreadsheet that works perfectly for me. What does this do that my spreadsheet doesn't?

A: Your spreadsheet can't tell you "you already own this" while you're browsing Instagram. It can't show your wishlist to a family member who wants to buy you a puzzle. It can't route you from "I want this" to "bought" in 30 seconds. If your spreadsheet is working, great — you can import it into MyPuzzleInventory via CSV and keep everything you've built. We're not asking you to start over.

### Q: Does this work for puzzles from small or indie brands? What about puzzles I got secondhand with no box?

A: At launch we cover the major brands that make up most collectors' shelves — Ravensburger, Buffalo Games, Cobble Hill, and White Mountain. Indie and specialty brands are coming, and you can request any missing puzzle directly from a search result. For secondhand puzzles without a box, you can add them manually with whatever details you have — title, piece count, a photo. It won't always match a catalog entry, but your collection stays complete.

### Q: Is there a mobile app, or is this desktop only?

A: MyPuzzleInventory is a responsive web app — it works on your phone's browser without downloading anything. The core use case (search a puzzle, check if you own it, add it to your wishlist) works in under 10 seconds on mobile. A React Native app is on the roadmap. For now: bookmark it, and it'll behave like an app.

## Internal FAQ

### Q: The scraper is the foundation of everything — catalog, SEO, affiliate links. What's your plan when Ravensburger blocks it?

A: Scraper blocking is expected, not exceptional — we architect for it from day one. Scrapers live in a separate repo from the app, run on a schedule, and output JSON that gets imported via API. When one breaks, the app keeps running on cached data. Mitigation layers: rotating user agents, rate limiting, off-peak scheduling. Longer term, we pursue official product feeds and affiliate API access from brands — one "yes" from a brand replaces their scraper entirely with cleaner data. Blocking a scraper is annoying; it's never a single point of failure.

### Q: IPDb has a 7–10x larger catalog and a head start on community trust. How do you win before they fix their UX?

A: We don't need to win the catalog race — we need to own the use case. IPDb is a database. MyPuzzleInventory is a collector's tool with a commerce layer. The "Buy" button is structurally unavailable to IPDb as a non-profit; adding it would require becoming something they've explicitly chosen not to be. Our window is 12–18 months to establish the affiliate/commerce identity before a well-funded alternative appears. Speed of catalog growth matters less than depth on the 4 anchor brands — we need to be the most complete source for Ravensburger, Buffalo Games, Cobble Hill, and White Mountain, full stop.

### Q: Affiliate revenue at 5,000 MAU is roughly $375/month. What's the actual path to financial viability?

A: This is a side project built for passive income — the financial bar is covering costs and eventually generating meaningful recurring revenue without requiring daily attention. At 5k MAU, affiliate revenue (~$375/mo) covers hosting and tooling. At 10k MAU, premium tier conversions (~$1,500/mo recurring) make it genuinely passive. At 20k MAU, brand sponsorships make it a real income stream. None of those numbers require a team, a VC, or a full-time commitment. The constraint isn't capital — it's catalog quality and SEO compounding over time. Both are achievable without outside investment.

### Q: What does the realistic build timeline look like working evenings and weekends?

A: The 4-week plan assumes full-time availability. Evenings and weekends realistically means 8–12 hours/week — budget 8–12 weeks for a launchable MVP. The first scraper will break at least once and take longer than expected. Honest milestone sequence: weeks 1–2 project setup and first scraper, weeks 3–4 puzzle pages and search, weeks 5–6 auth and collection tracking, weeks 7–8 affiliate links and duplicate warning, weeks 9–10 polish and SEO, weeks 11–12 soft launch. Don't announce a launch date until week 8.

### Q: Legal exposure — scraping, affiliate links, user data. What's the actual risk surface?

A: Three areas: (1) Scraping ToS — most brand sites prohibit automated access. Risk is cease-and-desist, not litigation, for a small project. Mitigation: respectful rate limits, pivot to official feeds fast. (2) Affiliate compliance — Amazon Associates and most programs have strict disclosure rules. The customer FAQ answer ("we earn a small referral fee") is compliant disclosure; read each program's ToS before signing up. (3) User data — GDPR applies to EU users, CCPA to California residents. A collection of puzzle titles is low-sensitivity, but email addresses require a real privacy policy before launch. Supabase Auth handles most compliance infrastructure.

### Q: What's the one thing that kills this completely?

A: The catalog never reaches critical mass. If 40% of collector searches return zero results in the first month, the duplicate-prevention use case doesn't work, word of mouth turns negative, and no UX polish recovers it. Everything depends on catalog depth on the brands collectors actually own. The kill condition: scrapers fail repeatedly, official feeds don't materialize, and community contributions don't fill the gap fast enough. Mitigation: treat the Ravensburger scraper as the most important piece of engineering in week 1, not an afterthought.

## The Verdict

### Concept Strength

MyPuzzleInventory survived the gauntlet in the places that matter most. The core use case is verified, the positioning is clean, the competitive gap is real, and the financial model is honest. The passive income framing is the best thing that came out of this process — it clarifies every scope decision downstream and removes the pressure to over-build.

The concept is ready to move to a PRD. But three things need deliberate attention before you write a single line of code.

---

### Forged in Steel

**The daily-use hook.** "You already own this" as a duplicate-prevention warning is not a feature — it's the reason someone opens the app every time they see a puzzle online. Every competitor is a journaling tool you use monthly. This is something you use daily. That frequency difference is the entire retention argument, and it survived every challenge.

**The Discogs positioning.** Clean, memorable, instantly communicates catalog + collection + commerce. No competitor owns this identity. It anchors every conversation about what the product is.

**The commerce layer as structural moat vs IPDb.** IPDb cannot add a commerce layer without becoming something they've explicitly chosen not to be. That's not a UX gap — it's a structural one. The 12–18 month window to establish the affiliate identity before a well-funded alternative appears is real and defendable.

**The passive income model.** Affiliate + premium tier + eventual brand sponsorships is a coherent, proven monetization stack for a niche catalog. The milestones are honest: costs covered at 5k MAU, genuinely passive at 10k MAU, meaningful income at 20k MAU. No VC, no team, no full-time commitment required.

**FAQ transparency.** The affiliate disclosure, the data portability commitment, the open data format promise — these are trust signals that most niche tools never bother with and that will pay dividends in a small community where word travels fast.

---

### Needs More Heat

**The onboarding moment.** The "magic moment" — 5+ puzzles added in the first 10 minutes — is described as critical but not designed. What does the empty state look like? How does the app suggest puzzles to add? What happens after puzzle #1 is logged? This is the highest-leverage UX investment in the product and it gets one sentence in the brief. Before the PRD, sketch this flow in detail.

**The zero-results experience.** "Help us add this puzzle" is the right mechanism but it's not designed. What does the prompt look like? Does the user get notified when it's added? Does it create a "pending" wishlist entry? This is a retention moment disguised as an error state — it needs to be treated as a feature, not a fallback.

**The premium tier.** "Nice-to-have features for $4/month" is a direction, not a product spec. Collection valuation, advanced export, lent-out tracking — none of these have been validated as things collectors will actually pay for. Before building premium, talk to 5 collectors and ask what would make them pay $4/month. The answer might surprise you.

---

### Cracks in the Foundation

**Amazon Associates ToS — unverified risk.** The affiliate model assumes Amazon Associates as the primary program. Amazon has specific, non-obvious rules for catalog and price-comparison style sites. This has not been verified. Spend 30 minutes reading the Operating Agreement before building the affiliate infrastructure — specifically section 5 on prohibited uses. If Associates doesn't work for this use case, the primary monetization model needs a different anchor retailer.

**The 5% affiliate click-through rate is an assumption, not a benchmark.** The revenue model is built on 5% of MAU clicking through to a purchase. This number comes from general affiliate marketing benchmarks, not puzzle-catalog data. It could be higher (collectors on a wishlist are high-intent buyers) or lower (they already know where they want to buy). Validate this in the first 30 days post-launch before building financial projections around it.

**Catalog quality is the kill condition and weeks 1–2 are the most dangerous.** The entire product depends on the Ravensburger scraper working reliably before anything else is built. "It will take longer than expected and break at least once" is in the brief. It needs to be in the build plan as the highest-priority, highest-risk item — not task 3 of 12 in week 1.

---

**Overall: Ready to proceed.** The cracks are fixable, not fatal. Address the Amazon ToS question before writing affiliate code. Design the onboarding flow before writing auth code. Treat the Ravensburger scraper as the first deliverable, full stop.
